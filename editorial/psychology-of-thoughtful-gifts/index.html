The gifting culture

The psychology of a truly thoughtful gift.

What makes someone feel seen — and why surprise is not everything.

VYRDICT Editorial · November 26, 2026

We often spend hours trying to surprise someone. But a gift can succeed in a quieter way: by showing that we listened. Research on gift exchange suggests that the giver and recipient do not always agree on what counts as thoughtfulness.

Listen before you surprise.

In a 2011 series of studies, researchers Francesca Gino and Francis Flynn found that recipients tended to appreciate explicitly requested gifts more than givers expected. A wish list is not necessarily the enemy of creativity. It can be evidence of what will genuinely make someone happy.

Think beyond the moment of unwrapping.

A gift may look spectacular for a few seconds yet become a burden to store or maintain. A well-chosen object that fits into the recipient’s routine can offer pleasure long after the occasion. Ask yourself whether they have the space, time and desire to use it.

Sometimes the best gift is an experience.

Research by Cindy Chan and Cassie Mogilner found that experiential gifts strengthened relationships more than material gifts in the exchanges they studied. A concert, ceramics class or afternoon together might be more meaningful than another object — provided the recipient enjoys it and can actually attend.

Start with who they are, not what you want their reaction to be.

There is no single scientifically perfect gift. These studies describe patterns, not rules for every person. The most useful takeaway is to listen, respect preferences and choose something the recipient will enjoy on their own terms.
